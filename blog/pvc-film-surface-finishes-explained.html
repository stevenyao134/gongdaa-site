PVC Film Surface Finishes: Super Matte, Gloss, Embossed and Synchronised

Published: November 10, 2026  |  By GONGDA Team

Two films can carry the identical printed design and behave like entirely different products, because the difference is not the picture - it is the finish. Gloss level, texture and topcoat decide how the surface reads in light, how it feels in the hand, how it fingerprints and how it cleans. Finish is where buyers form opinions, and it is the specification line most often left vague.

This guide explains what each finish family actually is, how the textures are produced, where each one performs, and what maintenance each one asks of the end customer.

What 'Finish' Actually Consists Of

A film finish is three decisions made together:

  • Gloss level - how much light the surface reflects, from super-matte through silk to high gloss.
  • Texture - the physical emboss pressed into the film, from a fine skin pore to a deep registered grain.
  • Topcoat - the surface chemistry that adds scratch resistance, anti-fingerprint behaviour and cleanability.

Specify only the design and colour and you have specified a third of the surface. The other two thirds are where the product's feel and its service life live.

Texture is pressed into the film at production - it is part of the product, not a coating added later.

How Embossing Works

Texture comes from an engraved roller pressing the pattern into the film as it is produced. Two levels exist in the market:

  • Stock textures use rollers the factory already holds - skin pores, linear grains, stone stipple. They carry no tooling cost and are available on short lead times, which makes them the right choice for most programmes.
  • Custom embossing means a new engraved roller - a real one-off tooling investment, justified for a signature surface a brand wants to own. Our customisation guide covers when that investment pays back.
  • Synchronised embossing registers the texture to the printed grain, so ridges fall exactly on the printed grain lines. It is the most convincing wood-look surface available on film, and the reason premium programmes specify it: at a metre's distance, sight and touch agree the surface is timber.

Gloss in Practice

Gloss is the finish decision with the most installation consequences. A high-gloss surface reflects the room - which means it also reflects every wave in the core, every grain raised in the substrate and every fingerprint in a hallway. The practical rules:

  • High gloss demands genuinely flat substrates; on a slightly waved panel the finish turns the defect into a mirror.
  • Matte and super-matte finishes hide substrate imperfection, which is why they dominate large flat areas like wall panels.
  • Fingerprint behaviour is a topcoat property, not a gloss property - our soft-touch films resist it specifically, which is why touch-height surfaces specify them.

Under showroom lighting, gloss sells the programme - and on site, it audits the substrate.

Matching Finish to Application

  1. Kitchen fronts: gloss or super-matte for the premium tier, silk for volume; pair with the adhesive and edge decisions in our kitchen guide.
  2. Wardrobes and bedrooms: super-matte and soft-touch dominate; the tactile finish photographs as quality and forgives handling.
  3. Commercial and hospitality: matte finishes for large surfaces, embossed textures where close contact matters, gloss only as a deliberate feature.
  4. Wood-look programmes: synchronised grain where the budget allows, stock emboss elsewhere - both are covered alongside the marble family in our wood grain vs marble comparison.

One specification habit prevents most finish disputes: approve the finish on a full-size sample, in the light of the actual room, before production. Finish is a product property you buy with your eyes and your hands - so sample with both.
