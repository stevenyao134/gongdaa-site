Fire Safety and Decorative PVC Film: What the Ratings Actually Mean

Published: November 10, 2026  |  By GONGDA Team

Fire questions arrive in this industry as one-line requirements - 'Class A surface', 'B-s1-d0 wall finish' - and they are the requirement category most often mishandled, in both directions. Buyers sometimes accept a claim that does not mean what the tender intended, and suppliers sometimes sign up to classifications their product was never tested for. Both failures are avoidable with a clear picture of how the ratings actually work.

This guide explains the two rating systems you will meet, why fire performance belongs to the assembly rather than the film, what a film supplier can honestly provide, and the wording that keeps a tender verifiable.

The Principle: The Rating Belongs to the Assembly

A decorative film is a facing measured in fractions of a millimetre. In a real wall or door, it sits on a substrate - MDF, WPC, calcium silicate, gypsum - and the substrate decides most of the fire behaviour of the finished panel. Fire test methods are written for products and assemblies, not for decorative skins, which is why the meaningful classification is the one earned by the finished composite:

  • A film applied to a non-combustible mineral core is part of an assembly that may achieve a high Euroclass.
  • The same film on a combustible MDF core cannot inherit that classification - the core governs.
  • Nobody can honestly sell 'a Class A film' in isolation, because the film alone is not the thing the rating describes.

That is not a limitation imposed by difficult suppliers - it is how the test standards are written. Any programme with a fire requirement should be specified as an assembly: core plus facing, tested as built.

Formulation controls how a film burns as part of an assembly - but the assembly is what gets classified.

EN 13501-1: The Euroclass System

European specifications use EN 13501-1, which classes construction products by reaction to fire. The classes most relevant to interior finishes:

ClassMeaningTypical products
A1Non-combustible - no contribution to fire at any stageStone, concrete, pure mineral boards
A2 / BVery limited / limited combustibility, with smoke (s) and droplet (d) sub-classesMineral composites, some treated boards - film-faced assemblies only if tested as built
C / DAcceptable contribution in defined building usesTimber, some composites; common in fit-out specifications
EResistance to a small flame onlyMany ordinary plastics-bearing products; the floor of acceptability where E is specified

The sub-classifications matter as much as the letter. s1, s2, s3 grade smoke production (s1 being the least), and d0, d1, d2 grade flaming droplets (d0 being none). A specification reading 'B-s1, d0' is demanding both limited combustibility and clean smoke behaviour - and the combination, not the letter alone, is what the tender is buying.

ASTM E84: The North American System

The Steiner tunnel test (ASTM E84) measures how far flame spreads across a sample relative to red oak and how much smoke it develops, producing two index numbers:

ClassFlame Spread IndexTypical use
Class A0 - 25Corridors and exits, most commercial interior finishes
Class B26 - 75General commercial interiors
Class C76 - 200Lower-traffic interior locations

Smoke Developed Index carries the same bands, and many US specifications cap it separately even when the flame-spread class is met. As with EN 13501-1, the test is performed on the product as it will be used - the finished assembly.

Formulation, not labels, determines behaviour in a fire - which is why testing, not certificates of intent, is the evidence that counts.

What a Film Supplier Can and Cannot Honestly Provide

Here is the honest position, and it is the position any credible supplier should take:

  • Can provide: third-party test reports on the film's own chemistry and emissions - ours are tested by SGS against REACH, RoHS 2.0 and phthalate limits, published on the certifications page; formulation details that matter to fire behaviour; and cooperation in arranging a fire test of the laminated assembly with your core.
  • Cannot honestly provide: an EN or ASTM classification for the film alone as though it were the finished panel, or a fire certificate for an assembly the supplier has never seen.
  • Should say clearly: that the fire claim belongs to the finished assembly, and that the core supplier's test report is the document to verify.

A supplier who volunteers a fire certificate for a decorative film in isolation is either misunderstanding the standard or selling reassurance rather than evidence. Both are reasons to read the report itself - which product was tested, in what assembly, and against which clause.

What to Write Into a Tender

  1. Name the standard and the class precisely: 'EN 13501-1, class B-s1, d0' or 'ASTM E84, Class A with SDI below X' - not 'fire rated'.
  2. State the assembly: core material and facing, so the classification is earned by what will actually be installed.
  3. Require the test report, not a summary: the report names the tested product and configuration.
  4. Assign responsibility: the core supplier evidences the core claim; the facing supplier evidences chemistry and emissions; the panel maker evidences the assembly if the assembly is what was classified.
  5. Match the requirement to the building use - over-specifying Class A everywhere is how projects end up substituting materials late and paying for it in programme time.

Red Flags

  • A 'Class A film' claim with no test report, or a report that tested a different product or configuration.
  • A fire classification quoted for a film-faced panel where only the bare core was tested.
  • Willingness to 'adjust the certificate' rather than the product - the clearest signal in this whole category.
  • Smoke sub-classes dropped from a quote because 'the letter class is what the tender checks'.

Fire compliance, like the rest of the regulatory stack - REACH, formaldehyde, CBAM covered in our sustainability guide - rewards buyers who ask for reports and punishes buyers who accept adjectives.
