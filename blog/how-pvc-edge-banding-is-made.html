How PVC Edge Banding Is Made: From Pellets to Coiled Tape

Published: November 18, 2026  |  By GONGDA Team

An edge banding strip looks simple - a few millimetres of decorated plastic - but the difference between an edge that survives five years of cabinet doors and one that whites up at the corners is decided at five or six specific moments in production. Buyers who understand those moments write better specifications and read supplier samples more accurately.

Here is the full line, stage by stage, as it runs in our factory.

Stage 1: Formulation

Everything starts as pellets and powder: PVC resin, plasticisers that give the strip its flexibility, stabilisers, and the pigment base that will carry the colour. The formulation is tuned to the gauge - a 0.4mm edge needs different flexibility than a 3mm thick strip for panel ends - and to the duty: furniture-grade edges are formulated to flex with the board through seasonal humidity swings without cracking.

Formulation is where flexibility, stability and colour base are decided - everything downstream inherits these choices.

Stage 2: Extrusion

The compound is extruded through a die as a continuous strip, calibrated to gauge and cooled on a conveyor. What matters here is dimensional consistency: thickness within tolerance along the whole run, because an edge that varies will feed differently through the customer's machine and bond unevenly. This is also where strip flatness is set - a bowed strip fights the feed wheels and shows as glue-line waviness after pressing.

Stage 3: Printing

The design layer - wood grain, stone, solid colour - is printed onto the strip with roller stations. Our ink systems are covered in detail in the ink comparison guide; the production-relevant point is registration: on a grain design, every metre of the roll must carry the pattern consistently or the edge will not match its neighbouring panels after the boards are cut.

Stage 4: Embossing

Texture is pressed in with an engraved roller - a fine skin pore, a linear grain or a deeper wood texture. On synchronised designs the emboss registers to the printed grain, ridge to line, which is what makes an edge read as timber rather than as photograph. Emboss depth also has a practical side: it changes how the edge scuffs, and how visible those scuffs are.

Winding tension and packing protect the work of every upstream stage - a dented roll is a rejected roll.

Stage 5: Topcoat

A clear topcoat adds scratch resistance and sets the surface gloss - super-matte through high gloss, per the programme. The topcoat is also the layer the hot melt actually grips, so its chemistry and its cleanliness are adhesion-critical. A contaminated or under-cured topcoat shows up weeks later as delamination on the customer line, which is why topcoat control is one of our standing QC checkpoints rather than a spot check.

Stage 6: Slitting and Winding

The wide strip is slit into edging widths - 22mm, 35mm, and the rest of the range from 0.4 to 3mm gauge - and wound into rolls or packed into cartons. Slit-edge quality matters: a burred or stretched slit edge will not sit flush against the panel. Winding tension and packaging protect the coil through shipping; dents and flat spots on a roll become feed problems on the customer machine.

The QC Points That Decide the Product

  1. Gauge scan along the run - feed consistency downstream depends on it.
  2. Colour against the master panel - under the same light source the customer will use, not just under factory daylight.
  3. Emboss and gloss uniformity - raking light across the strip.
  4. Adhesion test - the topcoat is what the glue grips; we test bond on the line, and the customer-facing method is in our adhesive guide.
  5. Flatness and slit edge - checked per roll before packing.

None of this is exotic - it is disciplined repetition. But it is why two edges that look identical on a sample card can behave completely differently after six months of pressing.
